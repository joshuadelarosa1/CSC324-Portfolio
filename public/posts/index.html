Process

Process

We used practices like sprints, frequent standups/checkins, and an issue backlog. Through using sprints, we were able to better define what work needed to be done within the specified time frame, and who exactly would contribute to reaching that goal. Frequent standups/checkins allowed us to have a clear picture in the progress that was actually being made vs what was expected. We used the issue tracking system in GitHub also for having a clear idea of the progress we were making overall. The rest of my group did a very good job of following through on using these processes throughout the semester. I had moments where I could have improved on my follow-through, especially during the second half of the semester. Self Learning

Self Learning

For our tech tutorial we discussed Lua, our “technology”, and Löve, the framework for our technology. Lua was the programming language we used to code our game. We used Löve as the basis to compile our game and get access to important functions (like love.graphics). It is especially useful for creating 2D games where it is important to easily see/test both the front-end and the back-end. What is lacks is more extensive testing capabilities, especially when it comes to the front-end of a game. When comparing with Unity, we realized that Lua was more suited to what our needs were. While Unity provided a lot of functionality for 3D objects, we wanted our game to be 2D.

Ideation

Ideation

This snippet is located in our Design repository, under the file “CSC324 Needfinding and Ideation.pdf”. We all worked on ideation together as a group, and Connor wrote out our process for us (thank you Connor).

In terms of techniques, we used multiple methods of ideation. Specifically, we employed round-robin pitch ideas, reference game benchmarking and team goal alignment. After conducting our needfinding and discovering what users would like to see in a mobile game, we came up with ideas which would fufill those needs. I originally pitched a puzzle game, similar to one we had seen in class, but realized that it would be hard to incorporate Grinnell specific aspects to the game and also hard to gage difficulty level for a variety of users. Gabi and I then worked together to pitch the idea of having a Grinnell life simulator. We used ‘BitLife’ as our ‘reference game’ which would help us give the gist of what we were going for.

Needfinding

Needfinding

In order to discover users’ needs, we conducted interviews to gauge what users like and dislike about mobile games. From this process, we learned that users really value the visual looks and feel of mobile games. They also enjoy having a story aspect/motivation to continue playing the game. Balancing the difficulty of the game was also very vital in keeping users hooked on playing a mobile game. One interview, in particular, also gave us the other side of the spectrum as a user who does not really like playing mobile games. For this interviewee, the most important aspect of a mobile game was being something that is mentally stimulating, but also quick to do during the day (like NYT games).
